# 2026 Late Planting Risk Video Research Pack

Working thesis: the publishable story is not "crop disaster already." The defensible story is that the 2026 spring start has compressed the growing season across parts of Western Canada and the northern U.S., pushing crop stages into higher-risk heat, disease, and fall frost windows. That distinction matters.

## 3. Satellite / GEE Evidence Lane

Use satellite evidence as visual corroboration, not as the primary truth source.

Best near-term layers:

- MODIS Terra/Aqua snow cover / NDSI: good for broad snow confirmation at 500 m; NASA says MODIS near-real-time products are generally available within 60 to 125 minutes after observation, but cloud and daytime clear-sky constraints matter.
- Google Earth Engine `MODIS/061/MOD10A1` and `MODIS/061/MYD10A1`: daily global 500 m snow-cover products; good for dated snow maps if the acquisition date is stamped on screen.
- Sentinel-2: higher visual quality for snow/wet fields but optical, so cloud cover can make the "latest clear image" days or weeks old.
- Sentinel-1 SAR: better for wetness/standing water under cloud, but harder to explain and not a direct "seeded/unseeded" detector.
- Existing Bushel Board posture: the repo currently treats weather, drought, satellite, and GEE-derived lanes as context only until individually admitted. Keep that guardrail.

Video rule: every satellite frame needs the acquisition date, product name, and a plain label such as "snow/wetness proxy, not seeded-acre proof."

Implication: a May 20 to June 5 seeding window does not just reduce time; it shifts sensitive stages into less forgiving weather:

- Canola: flowering gets pushed toward hotter July conditions; maturity and green seed risk move closer to September frost.
- Wheat/barley/oats: heading, flowering, fusarium head blight risk, grain fill, and drydown move later.
- Peas/lentils: flowering heat risk rises; peas seeded after the third week of May usually see reduced yield potential in Manitoba guidance.
- Corn/soybeans: maturity before frost becomes the gating issue, especially on longer-season hybrids/varieties.

## 5. Cold Soil, Snow, and Seed Depth

### Soil temperature thresholds

Saskatchewan Agriculture and Manitoba Agriculture agree on the broad ranking:

| Crop group | Practical temperature read |
| --- | --- |
| Wheat/barley/oats | cold tolerant; around 4-5 C can work, but emergence slows |
| Peas/lentils | about 5 C; peas/faba beans more cold tolerant than lentils |
| Canola/mustard | can germinate very cold, but fast emergence is better at warmer temperatures; Manitoba flags 8-10 C as important for canola speed through higher-risk stages |
| Flax | about 3-4 C minimum, but Manitoba lists 9 C as the warmer germination target |
| Soybean/dry bean/kabuli chickpea | wait for about 10 C |
| Corn | Manitoba says germination does not occur below 10 C; optimum emergence at 12-15 C at 4-inch depth |

### Snow on seeded acres

Canola-specific evidence:

- If canola seed has not germinated and remains firm, snow/cold usually delays it rather than kills it.
- Snow can insulate if it arrives before the hardest freeze.
- Hardened early canola seedlings have survived roughly -8 C to -12 C in cited prairie studies; later, unhardened seedlings can be killed around -3 C to -4 C.
- Do not reseed immediately after a frost/snow event; Canola Council and NDSU both advise waiting several days to assess regrowth.

Cereal-specific evidence:

- Wheat, oats, and barley have the growing point below ground until after the three-leaf stage, so early vegetative frost injury is often leaf burn rather than plant death.
- Risk rises sharply once the growing point moves above ground at jointing.

### Seed depth

Rule for a shortened/cold season: do not chase depth unless you need moisture. Faster, uniform emergence is the goal.

Manitoba guidance:

| Crop | Depth guidance |
| --- | --- |
| Wheat/oats | 1.5-3 inches; only deep enough to reach moisture |
| Barley | 1.5-2 inches; deeper than 2 inches not recommended |
| Corn | 1.5-2 inches ideal; can go to 3 inches for moisture, but cold deep soil delays emergence |
| Canola | 0.5-1.25 inches; ideally 1 inch; do not chase moisture deeper than 1.25 inches |
| Flax | 1-1.5 inches |
| Sunflowers | 1.5-2 inches ideal; up to 3 inches in lighter soil |
| Peas | 1.5-2 inches; seed into moisture |
| Soybeans/dry beans | 0.75-1.5 inches; deep planting can delay and uneven emergence |

## 6. Late-Planting Effect by Crop

| Crop | Main risk from late start | Highest-risk stage now shifted later |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Canola | heat blast during flowering; pod abortion; fall frost causing immature/high-green seed; flea beetle/seedling disease if emergence drags | cotyledon to 2-leaf for frost; flowering for heat; seed fill/maturity for frost |
| Spring wheat | lower tillering, later heading, higher FHB/leaf rust exposure, later grain fill/drydown | jointing to heading; flowering; soft dough/maturity |
| Barley | reduced tillering and later drydown; malting quality risk if harvest weather turns poor | heading/flowering; maturity |
| Oats | barley yellow dwarf and crown rust risk increase in late seedings | vegetative-to-heading disease window; maturity |
| Field peas | reduced yield if seeded after third week of May; heat during flowering | flowering/pod set |
| Lentils | cool-tolerant start, but late flowering/fill pushes harvest risk | flowering/pod fill |
| Soybeans | June planting lowers yield potential and may force earlier maturity varieties; fall frost before full maturity hits yield/quality | R5-R6 seed fill through R7 maturity |
| Corn | cold wet planting causes stand issues; late planting increases failure to reach maturity and wet harvest risk | emergence uniformity; silking/pollination; black layer before frost |

## 8. Historical Analogues

### 2019: late/cool crop plus bad fall

Why it matters: this is the warning case. A crop that is late or uneven can still yield decently, but a wet/cold fall turns lateness into quality loss and unharvested acres.

Evidence:

- Canadian Grain Commission reported the 2019 canola crop was one to two weeks behind by late August.
- By mid-September 2019, barely 10% of canola had been harvested versus over 40% the prior year.
- First snow hit Alberta and parts of Saskatchewan in late September, followed by killing frost; Manitoba saw significant Thanksgiving-week snow.
- Final 2019 canola harvest issues remained: Saskatchewan overall 91% harvested by Nov. 18, but 19% still in fields in east-central Saskatchewan; Alberta only 84.5% harvested as of Dec. 3, with 34% of Peace and 20% of North East seeded canola still in fields.
- MASC annual report said Manitoba had a record 417,000 acres of unharvested insured crop by end of November, mainly wheat, canola, and grain corn.

### 2022: late/wet spring but cooperative mid-season/fall

Why it matters: this is the "do not overclaim" case.

Evidence:

- MASC said Manitoba's spring 2022 was among the wettest in recent memory; by end of May only 40% was seeded versus 92% five-year average.
- More than 860,000 acres were reported unseeded due to excess moisture.
- After June, warm/timely growing conditions allowed many late-seeded crops to establish well.
- MASC reported average-to-above-average yields in many regions and few quality concerns for most cereals, pulses, and oilseeds, despite the late start.
- Exception: canola struggled in wet emergence conditions with intensified flea beetle pressure; more than 85,000 acres were reseeded.

Video takeaway: 2019 says late crop plus bad fall is dangerous. 2022 says late start plus cooperative summer/fall can still work. The forecast and frost window decide how much this becomes a yield/quality story.

## 9. Fall / ENSO Outlook

Current official NOAA ENSO update, May 14, 2026:

- ENSO status: El Nino Watch.
- El Nino is likely to emerge soon: 82% chance in May-July 2026.
- NOAA gives 96% chance it continues through December-February 2026-27.
- NOAA explicitly says peak strength is still uncertain and no strength category exceeded 37% chance in that update.
- Strong El Nino does not guarantee strong local impacts; it only shifts probabilities.

NOAA seasonal outlook discussion available at time of this pack was still the April 16, 2026 long-lead discussion:

- For May-July 2026, CPC favored above-normal temperatures over much of the U.S., but not strongly over parts of the northern Plains, Upper Mississippi Valley, and Great Lakes.
- CPC noted El Nino impacts become more reliable from July-September onward after the spring predictability barrier.
- By autumn, CPC said outlooks begin to resemble El Nino composites: warmer northern tier and expanding dryness across parts of the northern tier later in fall/winter.

Canada:

- ECCC seasonal forecasts are probabilistic, updated monthly, and should be re-checked. The ECCC system itself warns that white/equal-chance areas are more common for precipitation and at longer leads because skill declines.

Video framing: "El Nino risk is real and rising, but the fall forecast is a probability map, not a promise. The agronomic risk is that a late crop now depends more heavily on a cooperative September and October."

## 10. What This Means for Yield Estimates

Do not present a single yield-loss number yet. Present scenarios:

| Scenario | Weather path | Likely effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Catch-up | warm, dry enough to seed quickly; timely June rain; open September/October | acreage gets planted; yield drag limited; quality mostly preserved |
| Compressed season | planting finishes late May/early June; July heat; normal first frost | canola/pulses lose flowers/pods; cereals lose tillering/fill; more tough/damp harvest |
| 2019-style failure mode | late crop plus wet/cold September or early killing frost | green canola, low bushel weight, sprouting/weathering, drying costs, unharvested acres |
| 2022-style save | late planting, but warm/moist mid-season and open fall | yields can still be average to above average, but reseed/emergence costs can be high |

Quantitative anchors:

- NDSU small grains: average yield loss of roughly 1% per day after optimum planting date; after June 1 in North Dakota, wheat/barley replant yield may be reduced by about 50%.
- Manitoba grain corn: average yield reduction of about 1 bu/ac/day when seeding is delayed beyond mid-May.
- UMN soybeans: after May 30, soybean yield tends to drop about 1% per day or more as planting is delayed.
- MSU corn/soy: from mid-to-late May, corn and soybean both about 0.3%/day decline; in June, corn about 1.1%/day and soybean about 0.6%/day.

Use these as agronomic risk anchors, not as a direct forecast for Western Canada without local crop/region calibration.

## 11. X / Farmer Account Evidence

Use farmer X posts as field-level colour, not as a statistical dataset.

Rules before using:

- Capture handle, date/time, location, and original link.
- Prefer posts with field/town/RM/county location.
- Do not infer seeded acres from a snow photo.
- If a claim is reputational or tied to a named farm/business, ask permission or blur where necessary.
- Pair every X example with an official or satellite layer nearby in time and geography.

Safer phrasing: "official reports and farmer field posts both show pockets of remaining snow/cold/wet ground," not "farmers prove the crop is in trouble."

## 12. Recommended Video Structure

1. Hook: "This is not a disaster call. It is a timing-risk call."
2. Show official progress map/table: AB 20%, SK 16%, MB 13%, plus U.S. northern-state crop splits.
3. Show acreage scale: Canadian canola/wheat/pulses and U.S. corn/soy/spring wheat.
4. Explain the mechanism: late seeding compresses the crop calendar.
5. Crop-by-crop risk board: canola, spring wheat, barley/oats, peas/lentils, corn/soy.
6. Snow/cold soil section: what snow does to seed vs emerged plants; why soil temperature matters more than air temperature.
7. Satellite section: dated MODIS/Sentinel/GEE snapshots with caveats.
8. Historical contrast: 2019 bad fall vs 2022 cooperative fall.
9. Fall/El Nino section: rising El Nino odds but uncertain local impact.
10. Conclusion: "The risk is not that May is late. The risk is that late May moves the crop's most fragile stages into a tighter weather window."

## 13. Publication Guardrails

- Do not say "Western Canada crop failure."
- Do not say "satellite confirms unseeded acres" unless a seeded-acre model has been validated.
- Do not imply X posts are representative without a sampling method.
- Do not use "El Nino means drought/frost" as a direct forecast.
- Separate official progress, field anecdotes, satellite proxies, agronomic mechanisms, and forecast probabilities.
- Flag that insurance deadlines are not the same as best agronomic seeding dates.

## 15. Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview Check

- Do not equate late planting with guaranteed yield loss.
- Stamp satellite imagery with exact acquisition dates and distinguish optical imagery from SAR.
- Keep crop-specific windows separate; do not apply one crop's deadline/risk to another crop.
- Evaluate drying days, not just warm forecasts.
- Historical analogues need caveats for modern seeding equipment and genetics.
